# Quantum Key Distribution and Quantum Networks Explained

**What is QKD:** Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) is a family of methods that lets two remote parties, conventionally called Alice and Bob, build a shared random secret key. Its security rests on physical principles, such as the disturbance caused by measuring an unknown quantum state and the impossibility of copying one perfectly, rather than on the assumed difficulty of a mathematical problem. QKD does not carry the message itself, because it only produces key material that a separate cipher, such as a one-time pad or a symmetric algorithm, later uses to protect the data. The parties need a quantum channel for the signals and a classical channel that is public but authenticated, since without authentication an impostor could simply pose as the other party. In a real device, security also depends on how closely the hardware matches the assumptions of the theoretical proof.

**BB84:** BB84 is a QKD protocol, proposed in 1984, that follows a prepare-and-measure design in which the quantum states originate with Alice. In each round, Alice picks a random bit and a random basis, then sends Bob a single quantum state encoding that bit, typically in a photon's polarization or phase. In each round, Bob measures the incoming state in a randomly chosen basis, without knowing Alice's choice. Afterwards, the parties publicly compare their basis choices but not their outcomes, and they keep only the rounds in which the bases match, a step called sifting. To check security, they reveal a random sample of the sifted bits and estimate the error rate, because an eavesdropper cannot learn the states without risking detectable errors.

**E91:** E91 is a QKD protocol, proposed in 1991, that follows an entanglement-based design in which the quantum states originate with a shared source. In each round, the source creates one entangled pair of particles, then sends Alice one particle and Bob the other, typically photons correlated in polarization. In each round, Alice and Bob each measure their particle in a randomly chosen basis, without knowing the other's choice. Afterwards, the parties publicly compare their basis choices but not their outcomes, and they keep only the rounds in which the bases are compatible, a step called sifting. To check security, they reveal the outcomes of the remaining rounds and test a Bell inequality, because a strong violation means the pairs are highly entangled with each other and, since entanglement is monogamous, only weakly with any eavesdropper.

**QBER:** The quantum bit error rate (QBER) is the fraction of sifted bits on which Alice and Bob disagree, estimated by publicly comparing a random sample that is then discarded. Even with no eavesdropper it is non-zero, because of detector noise, optical misalignment and imperfect sources, and well-tuned systems commonly operate at values of a few percent. A QBER increase matters because the protocol cannot tell whether errors came from the environment or from an attacker, so the security analysis conservatively attributes all of them to an eavesdropper. Every additional error therefore costs more bits to correct and implies more leaked information, which shrinks the final key. Above a threshold the standard analysis yields no secret key and the session is aborted; for BB84 with one-way post-processing this threshold is approximately 11%, and the exact value depends on the protocol and the proof assumptions.

**Intercept-resend attack:** In an intercept-resend attack, an eavesdropper called Eve measures each intercepted signal in a randomly chosen basis and then sends Bob a fresh state matching the result. Eve picks the wrong basis about half the time, and in those sifted rounds the resent state makes Bob's result disagree with Alice's bit with probability one half. In the idealized case where every signal is attacked, this produces a QBER increase of about 25 percentage points, and attacking only a fraction of the signals produces a proportionally smaller rise. Each compared bit then exposes a full attack with a probability of roughly one quarter, so even a modest sample reveals it with near certainty. This attack is a textbook demonstration of how eavesdropping leaves a statistical trace, although it is far from the strongest strategy that security proofs must consider.

**Decoy states:** Practical transmitters often use attenuated laser pulses rather than true single-photon sources, so a pulse occasionally contains more than one photon. Those multi-photon pulses open the door to the photon-number-splitting attack, in which an eavesdropper keeps one photon and forwards another without disturbing the encoded state. In the decoy-state method, Alice randomly varies the intensity of each pulse among a few levels and announces which level was used only after transmission. An eavesdropper cannot tell from a pulse with a given photon number which level it came from, so any photon-number-dependent tampering shows up as inconsistent detection statistics across the levels. This comparison lets the parties bound how much of the signal really came from single-photon pulses, which greatly improves the key rate attainable over lossy channels.

**Post-processing:** Post-processing is the classical stage that turns the raw, noisy, partly exposed bit strings from the quantum exchange into a usable key. It starts with sifting and parameter estimation, in which the parties discard incompatible rounds and estimate how much an eavesdropper could know. Error correction, using techniques such as Cascade or LDPC codes, then makes the two strings identical at the cost of revealing some information over the public channel. Privacy amplification follows, applying a randomly chosen hash function that compresses the corrected string into a shorter one about which an eavesdropper has negligible knowledge. Every message in this stage must be authenticated, which consumes a small amount of previously shared secret key, so QKD is better described as key growing than as key creation from nothing.

**Secret key rate vs distance:** The secret key rate is the number of final secret bits produced per second or per transmitted pulse, and it falls steeply as distance grows. In standard telecom optical fibre at wavelengths near 1550 nm, loss is approximately 0.2 dB/km, so only about 1% of the light survives every 100 km. Because quantum signals cannot simply be amplified, this exponential loss translates into a rate that, for a direct link with no intermediate stations, cannot scale better than linearly with channel transmittance. At long distance the falling signal eventually becomes comparable to detector noise, the error rate climbs, and the key rate drops abruptly to zero at a maximum range. Depending on the hardware and protocol, useful rates over a direct fibre link are typically limited to distances on the order of a hundred to a few hundred kilometres, so these figures should be read as approximate.

**Trusted nodes:** A trusted node is an intermediate secure station that lets QKD span a distance longer than any single link can manage. The total distance is divided into a chain of shorter links, and each node runs QKD separately with its neighbours to obtain a local key on each side. To pass a key along, a node encrypts it with the next link's key, typically by a bitwise XOR, so the end-to-end key hops across the chain one link at a time. The weakness is that each node briefly holds the end-to-end key in readable form, so anyone who compromises a node learns it without causing any quantum-level disturbance. This approach works with today's hardware and can cover essentially any distance, but security then depends on physically protecting every node along the way.

**Quantum repeaters & satellites:** Quantum repeaters and satellites both aim to extend quantum links across distances that plain fibre cannot cover, because the no-cloning theorem rules out classical-style signal amplification. A repeater divides the distance into shorter segments, creates entanglement on each one, stores it in quantum memories, and joins the segments through entanglement swapping, with purification used to improve quality. In principle this gives end-to-end entanglement without any station needing to know the key, but memories and operations of the required quality remain an open research challenge, so practical repeaters are not yet available. Satellites take a different route, sending photons through free space, where most of the path is near-vacuum and loss grows far more slowly with distance than the exponential attenuation in fibre. They can reach intercontinental distances, although they are constrained by weather, background light, limited pass windows and, when a satellite holds the key itself, the need to trust that satellite.

**Single-photon detectors:** Single-photon detectors register the arrival of individual photons, and they are often the component that most limits a QKD receiver. Their main figures of merit are detection efficiency, dark count rate, dead time and timing jitter, which describe how often a photon is registered, how often a false click appears, how long recovery takes and how precisely arrival is timed. Semiconductor avalanche photodiodes, commonly InGaAs at telecom wavelengths, are compact and need only modest cooling, but they usually offer efficiencies of roughly 10 to 30% and can suffer from afterpulsing. Superconducting nanowire detectors can exceed roughly 80% efficiency with very low dark counts, at the price of cryogenic operation at a few kelvin. Detectors are also a known attack surface, since bright-light blinding has been shown to control some of them, which motivated designs such as measurement-device-independent QKD.

**Monitoring & anomaly detection:** Operational QKD systems stream telemetry such as QBER, sifted and secret key rates, detector count rates, optical power, polarization drift and temperature, which can be watched for signs of trouble. A QBER increase on its own does not reveal its cause, since an attack, a drifting component or a fibre disturbance can look similar, so correlating several signals over time is more informative than a fixed alarm on one metric. Because labelled examples of real attacks are scarce, unsupervised methods trained mostly on normal behaviour are a natural fit. An Isolation Forest builds random decision trees and flags points that are separated from the rest after unusually few splits, while an Autoencoder learns to reconstruct normal telemetry and flags samples with a large reconstruction error. These models support diagnosis and maintenance but do not replace the security analysis, because the guarantees on the key come from the protocol's proof and not from an anomaly score.
